Dana tries keeping her deals in a personal CRM app built for her
contacts — the kind that's good at remembering a friend's birthday.

Three weeks in, she gives up. A deal isn't a person. It's a moving
situation with four stakeholders and a story that changes on a call
nobody scheduled.

## The company CRM was never built to hold this part

Your company CRM is a good system of record. Stage, amount, close
date — the fields a forecast actually rolls up from.

It was never built to hold the part underneath that record. The exact
phrase the champion used to describe her boss's skepticism. The
competing internal project mentioned once, in passing, that turned out
to matter.

None of that fits a stage dropdown. All of it is the reason the deal
stalled, three calls later, when nobody could remember why.

## Personal CRM apps are built for your rolodex, not your deals

Search "personal CRM" and most of what turns up is built for a
different job — remembering how you met someone, when you last caught
up, a friend's kid's name.

That's a real need. It's just not this one.

A deal isn't a static contact with a birthday attached. It's several
stakeholders, a shifting story, and a timeline that moves whenever one
of them takes a call you didn't see coming.

## What "personal CRM" actually means for a sales rep

Strip the term down to what a rep actually needs and it's simpler than
the productivity-app version.

It's the read on a deal that lives underneath the official one — kept
somewhere that isn't just memory, and organized around the deal
instead of around one person's contact card.

Not a replacement for the company CRM. A layer under it, holding the
part the company system was never asked to hold.

## The same idea, one layer down from a second brain

This is a narrower version of [what we've written about a second
brain for sales reps](/blog/second-brain-for-sales-rep): a system
outside your own memory for everything a fifteen-deal pipeline is too
much to hold on its own.

A personal CRM is that idea applied specifically to the people in a
deal — not just what happened, but who said it, and what that person's
stake in the outcome actually is.

## What it has to do, to actually get used

Capture has to take seconds. Typing a structured entry loses to the
ninety seconds between calls every time — talking it through doesn't.

It has to organize around the deal on its own, because a rep mid-quarter
isn't building a filing system by hand.

And it has to surface the right stakeholder's history right before the
call where it matters, unprompted — not wait for the rep to remember
what she forgot and go looking for it.
