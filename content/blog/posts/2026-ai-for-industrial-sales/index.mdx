Dana walks the floor with a plant manager for forty minutes, standing
next to the conveyor line they're there to talk about. No laptop.
No signal past the loading dock. Nothing about that conversation will
ever touch her sales stack, because her sales stack assumes a screen
share.

## The industrial rep's day has almost nothing on a calendar

Most "field sales AI" gets built around a looser version of the office
day — meetings, just outdoors. Industrial sales isn't that.

The unit of work is the site visit, not the meeting. Badge in, walk the
floor, stand next to the equipment, badge out.

Phones often don't leave the truck past a safety line. Recorders never
do.

## What sales AI assumes, and what the floor actually is

| Sales AI assumes | The plant floor actually is |
|---|---|
| A scheduled video call | An unscheduled walk-through |
| A reliable signal | Dead coverage past the dock |
| One buyer in the room | Procurement, engineering, and safety, each separately |
| A screen to reference | A machine to stand next to |

Every row on the right is normal for an industrial rep. Every row on
the left is what most sales software still expects.

## Recording was never realistic here either

In plenty of verticals, "we don't record" is a positioning choice. On
a plant floor, it's closer to a fact of the environment — ambient
noise, safety restrictions on personal electronics near equipment,
facilities that don't allow phones past a badge line at all.

That's the same structural reality behind [sales AI for regulated
industries](/blog/sales-ai-for-regulated-industries): the fastest
answer to a compliance or safety question is having nothing that needed
capturing in the first place. Industrial sales gets there for
practical reasons, not policy ones — the noise alone would make a
recording useless.

## Where the deal actually gets built: before and after the walk

Take recording off the table and the real question is what a tool can
do around a motion built on foot, not on a keyboard. The answer is the
same shape as any field-heavy sale: the bookends.

Before the walk: what did the maintenance lead flag about downtime last
visit? What did procurement ask for in writing? A rep walking in
remembering that asks sharper questions than one rediscovering the
account cold.

After the walk: the debrief happens in the truck or the parking lot,
still in a hard hat sometimes, while the specific thing the plant
manager said about the conveyor's failure rate is still sharp. That
window is short.

We've made this case in general terms for [in-person sales
meetings](/blog/in-person-sales-meeting-ai): no software attends the
room, so the tool's job is prep and debrief. Industrial sales is a
sharper version of that argument, because the "room" often has no
signal for the tool to reach in the first place.

## The buying committee doesn't share a calendar either

A capital equipment decision on a plant floor moves through people who
rarely sit in the same meeting. The maintenance lead's concerns come up
standing next to the machine. Procurement asks about cost in a hallway
between two other vendors. A safety or ops lead weighs in on their own
schedule, sometimes weeks later.

Tracking that across sites — a rep covering a territory might carry a
dozen active accounts, each with its own version of this committee — is
a memory problem before it's a relationship problem.
