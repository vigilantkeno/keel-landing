Renee's exit interview says "comp." Her manager believes it, because
the number really was behind.

It's not why she left. She'd stopped believing the number was even
the point.

## The consensus: reps leave for money, territory, or a bad boss

Ask any VP of Sales why reps quit and you'll get the same three
answers. Comp is behind market. Territory got cut. The manager is
difficult.

All three are real. None of them is the whole list.

There's a fourth reason that almost never makes the exit interview,
because naming it sounds like an excuse: the rep got tired of being
watched.

## What monitoring actually converts selling into

Call scoring and conversation-intelligence tools were sold as coaching.
In practice, they turn every call into a performance review with a
one-week delay.

That's a different job than selling. Selling is judgment under
uncertainty — reading a buyer, adjusting mid-conversation, taking a
risk that might not pay off for months.

Being evaluated is different. It rewards the version of the call that
scores well, not the version that actually moves the deal.

A rep can do both for a while. Doing both indefinitely is what
burns people out — not the workload, the constant low-grade
self-monitoring underneath it.

## Why the best reps leave first

Here's the part that should worry a VP more than the exit interviews
do: monitoring doesn't burn out your average performers first. It
burns out your best ones.

Top reps have the most options. They're the easiest to place
somewhere else, and the first to notice when a tool implies distrust
of judgment they've already proven quarter after quarter.

A mediocre rep might welcome a scorecard — it's a clear bar to clear.
A rep who's been beating quota for two years experiences the same
scorecard as an insult dressed up as a feature.

That's the quiet mechanism. The monitoring stack installed to protect
revenue is, slowly, selecting for the reps easiest to control instead
of the reps hardest to replace.

## The tell: watch what reps do about it, not what they say

Nobody puts "I hated being scored" on a resignation letter. Watch
behavior instead of exit surveys.

Reps start keeping their real read of a deal somewhere the tool can't
see — a notes app, a personal notebook, a text to themselves after the
call. That's not a discipline problem.

It's a rep protecting the one honest account of the deal they still
have, because the sanctioned system stopped being safe for honesty the
moment it started grading them.

## What actually changes the outcome

The fix isn't removing structure. Reps benefit from reflecting on
their calls — the discipline is genuinely useful.

What has to change is who the reflection answers to. A rep who
processes a call for their own benefit, with nothing routing to a
scorecard or a manager, keeps the useful part and loses the part that
burns people out.

That's the actual distinction between coaching and surveillance: not
whether a tool watches the call, but whether the rep or the manager is
the one it was built for.

## The real cost isn't the reps who complain

The reps who complain about monitoring are, in a strange way, the
safer ones. They're still in the room, still telling you something's
wrong.

[The ones who go quiet are the ones to watch](/blog/gong-anxiety) —
the same anxiety that makes a rep perform for a scorecard is what
makes them stop raising their hand about it at all, right up until the
resignation letter.

Attrition from monitoring doesn't show up in a dashboard. It shows up
six months later, as a departure nobody quite saw coming — except,
looking back, everyone kind of did.
