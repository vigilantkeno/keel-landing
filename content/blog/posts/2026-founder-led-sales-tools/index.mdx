You close the deal, log the deal, and get back to building the thing you
just sold — all before your next call. Most sales software was built for
someone who only does the first part.

## The stack you're handed assumes a team you don't have

Every sales tool has a shape. An SDR feeds the pipeline. An AE runs it.
A manager reviews it Friday.

Founder-led sales has none of those roles. It has you, wearing all three
before lunch.

Buy the team-shaped tool anyway and you inherit its assumptions. A
dashboard built for a manager who doesn't exist. A setup flow that
expects a handoff between two people who are actually the same person.

## The four questions before you add anything

Run every tool through four questions before it joins the stack.

1. **Does it assume someone else is watching?** If a feature's value
   depends on a manager checking it, it's dead weight on a team of one.

2. **Does it need administering?** Every hour spent on permissions and
   field mapping is an hour not spent selling.

3. **Does it help you sell, or just catalog that you sold?** Logging a
   call isn't the same as being sharper on the next one.

4. **Does it survive your first hire?** The tool should stay useful the
   day someone else joins — not just be a habit you have to unwind.

A tool that fails two of these isn't a productivity investment. It's a
second job.

## The administration tax nobody prices in

Sales software gets sold on features, not on setup cost. Nobody puts
"four hours configuring stages you'll never look at again" on the
pricing page.

For a founder, that's the real price. Every hour spent mapping fields
is an hour not spent on the call that actually closes something.

The tools built for teams can absorb that cost — someone in ops owns
it. On a team of one, the founder owns it, on top of everything else
they already own.

## Why the "serious" stack is usually the wrong signal

There's a real instinct to buy the full enterprise sales stack early,
because it looks like what a real company runs. Founders do it to feel
credible, sometimes before they've closed a single deal on their own.

That instinct is backwards. A tool that assumes a team doesn't make you
look like you have one — it just makes you administer software built
for people who aren't there yet.

[Solo B2B sales](/blog/solo-b2b-sales) runs on the opposite premise:
everything collapses into one person's memory, one calendar, one set
of hours. The tools that help are the ones that respect that collapse,
not the ones pretending it hasn't happened.

## Where this eventually stops applying

The four questions have a shelf life. The day a founder hires a first
AE, some of the assumptions genuinely change — there's now someone else
to hand a deal to, and a real reason for a shared view of the pipeline.

[That transition is its own kind of hard](/blog/founder-to-first-ae),
mostly because the judgment a founder built up solo has to transfer
along with the accounts. But plenty of founders stay the whole sales
team by choice, indefinitely, and the tooling question above is the
one that actually matters for as long as that's true.
