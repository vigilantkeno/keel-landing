Following up stops being annoying the moment your message carries something
the prospect did not already have. "Just checking in" irritates people because
it is empty, not because it is frequent. That means the fix happens before you
open your email client — it happens in what you captured after the last
conversation, while you still remembered it.

Most advice on this treats *annoying* as a frequency problem. Send five times,
not eight. Wait four days, not two. That advice misreads the complaint. Nobody
has ever been irritated by a message that told them something useful.

## Annoying is an information problem

Think about the follow-ups you have received that did not bother you. Someone
answered a question you actually asked. Someone flagged a detail that changed
your thinking. Someone remembered a constraint you mentioned in passing and
came back with something that worked around it.

Now think about the ones that did bother you. They all had the same shape:
a pleasant sentence, a restatement of what you already knew, and a request for
a response you had no new reason to give.

The difference is not tone or timing. It is whether the sender had anything.

## Why reps end up with nothing to say

Not because they are lazy. Because the useful detail has a short shelf life
and the moment to record it is the worst possible moment.

You finish a call with a clear sense of what happened. The buyer hedged on
timing in a way that felt rehearsed. Someone mentioned a name you had not
heard before. You noticed you never got a straight answer about budget. All of
that is vivid for about twenty minutes.

Then the next call starts. By evening it has compressed to "good call, sending
pricing." By the time you sit down to write the follow-up, you have the summary
and none of the substance — so you write from the summary, and the summary
produces "just checking in."

The information was never missing. It decayed.

## What to capture so the follow-up writes itself

You do not need a formal process. You need to answer a few questions while the
conversation is still fresh — out loud is fine, and usually faster than typing:

- **What did they push back on that I smoothed over in my own head?**
  The objection you rationalized on their behalf is the one that kills the deal
  later.
- **Who did they mention that I have never spoken to?**
  New names are the single most reliable signal that the decision lives
  somewhere you are not.
- **What did I not get a straight answer to?**
  Not what they said — what they avoided saying.
- **If this slips two weeks, what actually breaks for them?**
  If nothing does, you do not have a deadline. You have a hope.

Every one of those produces a follow-up that writes itself, because each one
gives you something specific to say. "You mentioned Dana owns the security
review — should I put together something she can take into that, or would you
rather introduce us first?" is not annoying. It is useful, and it moves the
deal.

## What to do before your next follow-up

Take the last customer conversation you had. Answer the four questions above
from memory, honestly. If you cannot answer two of them, that is not a memory
failure — it is a signal about how the call actually went, and it is more
useful than any template.

Then write the follow-up from the answers rather than from the summary. It
will be shorter than the one you were going to send, and it will get a reply.
