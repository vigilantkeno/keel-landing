The moment I knew something was wrong with my workflow: a Tuesday
afternoon, reviewing my own recorded call, watching myself not ask the
question. The prospect had hinted their CTO was on the way out — the
single most important fact in that deal — and on the recording I watch
myself hear it, glance at the little red dot, and move to the next
discovery question. Some part of me had decided the follow-up was too
blunt for the transcript. I lost that deal four months later to the
incumbent, in a decision made by the incoming CTO I never built a
relationship with.

## Let me say the quiet thing first

I carried a bag in mid-market SaaS for years, and I hated being recorded.
Hated the disclosure at the top of the call. Hated the clips. Hated
knowing that any sentence I said could be replayed to an audience I
couldn't see, at a time I couldn't choose, without me in the room to say
what I'd been reading in the moment.

I want to be precise about why, because "rep hates recording" gets
dismissed as having something to hide. I had nothing to hide. I had
something to protect — the part of a sales conversation that only exists
when neither side is performing.

Recording didn't make me a worse person on calls. It made me a more
careful one. And careful, it turns out, is expensive. The candid
half-sentence, the blunt question, the moment you let a customer vent
past your agenda — the highest-value moves in my selling were exactly the
ones self-editing removed first.

## My customers were performing too

It took me longer to notice the other half. The disclosure that made me
careful made my customers careful.

Buyers are trained now. Legal has briefed them on recorded lines. The
real information — the budget politics, the internal skeptic, the
"between us, the timeline is fake" — arrived on my cell phone, at
dinners, in hallways after the official meeting ended. The recorded call
got the version of my customer that their procurement team would approve
of.

So the archive my company was paying for was, in a specific sense, an
archive of official positions. [The anxiety we named in an earlier piece](/blog/gong-anxiety)
runs both directions across the table. Everyone on a recorded call knows
it's a stage.

## The clip that finished the argument for me

The breaking point wasn't even my worst call. It was a decent one —
except for ninety seconds in the middle where I handled a pricing push
badly, and those ninety seconds ended up clipped in the team channel as a
coaching example. No context: not the three prior calls with that
account, not the customer's procurement history, not the recovery in the
last ten minutes that actually saved the relationship.

My teammates were kind about it. That wasn't the point. The point was
what I did on every call afterward: somewhere in my head, a small process
ran continuously, asking of each sentence — how would this look as a
clip?

You cannot sell well with that process running. You can sell safely.
Safe and good are different jobs.

## What I actually did instead, for years, badly

Here's my confession of workflow: I became a stairwell operator. Honest
conversations moved to my cell. Real deal notes lived in a personal doc
my manager never saw, because the CRM field was discoverable and the doc
wasn't. After big meetings I'd sit in the car and talk to myself for five
minutes — literally out loud, like a crazy person — because saying the
meeting back was the only way to find out what I actually thought about
it.

None of this was sanctioned. All of it worked better than the sanctioned
stack.

There was also the group chat — two reps I trusted, from two other
companies, where the honest pipeline reviews happened. "Tell me if I'm
crazy about this deal" produced better deal inspection than any forecast
call I ever sat in, because nobody in that chat could hurt me with the
answer.

The voice memo habit was the one that mattered. Some drives home I'd
catch the thing I'd missed in the room — the stakeholder who went quiet
after pricing, the commitment that was actually a deflection. Harvard
Business School later gave me the vocabulary for why:
[reflection at the end of work measurably improves performance](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/reflecting-on-work-improves-job-performance)
— the learning is in the processing, not the doing.

But a voice memo doesn't answer back. It never asked me the second
question. Nobody ever said: you've mentioned the champion going quiet
twice now — what's your actual evidence this deal is real?

## Why I didn't just buy a lighter recorder

When I finally went shopping, everything in the category was a variation
on the same machine: capture the call, transcribe it, summarize it,
store it. Cheaper versions of the thing I was avoiding, with friendlier
logos.

The problem was never which vendor held the recording. The problem was
the existence of the artifact. A recording is a thing that can be
replayed, scored, clipped, subpoenaed, shared upstream, and read without
context forever — [the scorecard problem](/blog/gong-scorecards) is just the most visible symptom
of artifact-thinking. Any tool built on the artifact inherits the
performance problem, no matter who owns the login.

What I wanted didn't exist: the thinking half of my stairwell workflow,
with the memory and the second question added, and the artifact removed.
A private sales AI in the plain meaning of both words — private, as in
mine; and for sales, as in built around how deals actually move.

## So we built Sara

Sara is the tool I needed on that Tuesday. She's a 24/7 AI deal assistant
you text or call after the moments that matter — the meeting, the lunch,
the drive home. No recording, no transcription, nothing joining your
calls. She works from what you tell her, which is the whole design: the
input is your read of the conversation, not a tape of it.

You talk; she asks the questions a sharp deal desk would ask. What did
the CFO actually commit to? You said the champion seemed off — off how?
What would have to be true for this deal to close this quarter, and
which of those things did today's meeting move?

And she remembers. The next time you mention that account — three weeks
later, in a parking lot — she knows what you said in March, what you
were worried about in May, and where your own story has shifted. My
personal doc never did that. My voice memos never did that.

## The first week is mostly uncomfortable questions

Here's what starting looked like for me, so you know what you'd be
signing up for. Day one, I talked her through my three shakiest deals on
the commute home — ten minutes each, just saying what I knew. Nothing
profound happened. Day three, after a renewal call, she asked me what the
customer's exact words had been about budget, and I realized I couldn't
say — I'd been listening to my own pitch instead of their answer. That
stung usefully.

Day five was the one I remember. Reviewing a deal I'd been calling
ninety percent for a month, she asked the deal-desk question: what has
the economic buyer personally said to you? The answer was nothing. I'd
never spoken to them. The deal was a fifty at best, and some part of me
had known it since April — the AI didn't find the truth, she just made it
impossible for me to keep not saying it.

An honest deal review without an audience turns out to be a different
activity from every deal review I'd ever done with one.

## What actually changed in my deals

The honest answer: my in-meeting behavior, first. Knowing the processing
would happen after — reliably, with a partner — let me stop
half-processing during. I stopped splitting attention between the
conversation and the fear of losing it. The blunt question came back,
because there was no transcript for it to look bad in.

The second change was compounding. An alternative to call recording
isn't a weaker record; it's a different one — a record of judgment
instead of audio. Six months of debriefs gave me something no archive
ever had: a history of what I believed and when, which is the raw
material of actually getting better. That's what private sales coaching
turned out to mean for me. Not tips. A witness to my own thinking, with a
long memory and no agenda.

## "But isn't your memory worse than a transcript?"

The objection I hear most, so let me take it head-on. Yes — a transcript
beats my recall of exact words. If verbatim is what your workflow needs,
use a recorder where it's legal and welcome, and go in peace.

But run the comparison on deal-relevant information rather than words.
The transcript holds everything said and nothing read: no body language,
no glance between stakeholders, none of the conversation before the
recording started or after it stopped, and nothing from the eighty
percent of a field relationship that never touches a scheduled call. My
debrief, done within the hour, holds my full read — thinner on verbatim,
richer on meaning — right when the research says reflection cements it.

And the AI closes the reliability gap from the other side. When my
account of a meeting is vague, she notices, and asks. A transcript never
once asked me what I actually meant.

## If you're a manager reading this over your rep's shoulder

A word for you, without the edge. I'm not arguing you should rip out
your conversation stack — for a scheduled-demo motion with a real
coaching practice behind it, it earns its seat. And nothing about Sara
competes with it, because she doesn't do its job. No recordings, no
analytics, nothing for you to review. That's the design, not a missing
roadmap item.

What I'd ask you to notice instead is the pattern I lived: which
conversations your reps take to their cell phones, which deals sound
different in the forecast call than they do in the hallway, where the
honest thinking on your team actually happens. Those migrations are
data. Your reps aren't hiding from accountability — they're protecting
the one workspace where they can think without performing.

The counterintuitive part, which took me years to see from the rep
side: a rep with a private place to be honest shows up more honest in the
shared places. The optimistic forecast survives on floors where there's
nowhere safe to say the pessimistic version first.
