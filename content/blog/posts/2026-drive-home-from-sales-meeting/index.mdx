The meeting ended eleven minutes ago. You're in the driver's seat,
key in the ignition, not turning it yet. Someone said something in
there that changed how this deal reads — a pause before "we're fine
on budget," a name mentioned once and never again.

Right now you can still hear exactly how they said it.

By the time you're at your desk, you'll remember that it happened.
You won't remember how.

## The habit everyone already has

Ask a sales team what good post-meeting discipline looks like.
You'll get the same answer everywhere: log it fast.

Open the CRM, fill the fields, move on before the next call swallows
the details. Speed is the whole strategy.

It's not bad advice. It's just aimed at the wrong problem.

Getting notes into a system quickly protects against forgetting that
a meeting happened. It does nothing to protect the part that
actually mattered — the tone, the hesitation, the thing that got
said almost by accident.

A CRM field asks what stage the deal is in. It never asks what you
noticed.

So the noticing gets left in the parking lot, and the system fills
up with stage changes instead of judgment.

## The window closes faster than people think

Insight from a meeting doesn't decay evenly. It's sharp for a few
minutes, blurry within the hour, and by the next morning it's a
summary of a summary.

The gist survives. The texture doesn't.

The drive home sits right inside that sharp window. Not because a
car is special — because it's the first unscheduled ten minutes
after the meeting ends, before the next thing starts pulling your
attention somewhere else.

Typing waits for a desk. A desk is usually forty minutes and three
Slack messages away.

## Why spoken beats typed here

Typing a note forces you to organize before you've finished
noticing. You're translating a felt sense — something's off with
this deal — into a field with a dropdown.

The translation strips out exactly the part worth capturing.

Saying it out loud doesn't ask for that translation yet. You can say
"something felt off when I brought up the renewal date" before you
know why. The why can come later.

The record just needs to hold the observation long enough for the
why to catch up to it.

That's the actual claim here. Not that talking is faster than
typing, though it is. Talking captures a kind of note typing
structurally can't — the one you haven't organized into a sentence
yet.

## What a drive-home debrief actually sounds like

It's not a report. It's closer to what you'd say to a colleague who
asked "how'd it go?" in the elevator right after.

A few things worth naming, every time:

What changed. Not what was discussed — what's different now than
before the meeting started.

Who said the thing that mattered. Not everything they said. The one
line that's still sitting with you.

What you'd do differently next time. Even one sentence. Especially
if it stings a little.

Three to five minutes covers all of it most days. Some days it's
ninety seconds.

If you want [the sharper, more private version of these
questions](/blog/how-to-reflect-on-a-sales-call), it's the same
window, asked a little more honestly.

The length isn't the point. Doing it before the next thing starts
is.

## The reflex that makes it stick

[We've written before about the moments that don't survive a meeting
bot](/blog/what-call-recording-cant-capture) — the hallway aside,
the lunch, the drive home.

This is the same idea from the other direction. The meeting is the
performance. The debrief right after is where the actual thinking
happens.

Skip it, and you're left with what got recorded — which was never
the part that mattered most.

Most reps don't skip the drive-home debrief because they don't
believe in it. They skip it because "I'll do it at my desk" is easy
to promise and easy to break.

By the time the desk arrives, the sharp version is already gone.

The habit only survives if it's easier than the alternative. Typing
later isn't easier. It's just later.
