Every B2B qualification checklist asks the same handful of questions.

Budget. Authority. Need. Timeline. Some version of MEDDIC bolted on top.

None of it filters out a weak lead on its own. A rep with a number to
hit can answer every field "yes" without lying, exactly once, about
each one.

## What the checklist actually measures

A qualification field doesn't measure the deal. It measures what the
person filling it in is willing to commit to paper.

"Budget confirmed" can mean a signed PO. It can also mean a prospect
who didn't flinch at a price mentioned once, in passing. Both fill the
same field the same way.

The checklist isn't lying to you. It's just being graded by someone
with a reason to round up.

## Four signals a field can't fake

These don't live on a standard scorecard. They live in what actually
happened before the call, and they're much harder to fake because
faking them takes effort, not just optimism.

**A forcing function that isn't yours.** Did the buyer already have a
deadline before you showed up — a contract expiring, a compliance
date, a budget that resets next quarter? If the only urgency in the
deal is the one you introduced, there's no clock running that you
don't control.

**One name, not a committee.** Can a single person say yes, or does
the honest answer start with "they'd have to check with"? A real
buyer already knows who signs. A hopeful one is still finding out.

**A paper trail that predates you.** Look for a doc, a Slack thread,
a budget line — anything showing the org was already working the
problem. [A lead you introduced the pain to](/blog/lost-deal-analysis)
is a much longer sale than one where you walked into a problem someone
already owned.

**Visible effort from the champion.** Did they set up the meeting
themselves? Loop in a stakeholder unprompted? Effort spent before you
asked for anything is the one signal a scorecard field genuinely
can't fake — it costs the champion something real to fabricate it.

## Why these get skipped

They're slower to check than a dropdown. A dropdown takes five
seconds. Confirming a real forcing function takes an actual question,
asked out loud, and a willingness to hear "not really" as the answer.

Most reps don't skip it out of laziness. They skip it because the
[fast version protects the pipeline number](/blog/deal-review-without-manager)
and the slow version sometimes shrinks it.
