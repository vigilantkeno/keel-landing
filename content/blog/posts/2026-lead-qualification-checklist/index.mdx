Marcus qualified the lead on a Tuesday.

Budget: confirmed. Economic buyer: named. Timeline: this quarter.
Champion: engaged.

Five weeks later the deal stalled. The budget owner had moved teams.
Nobody had checked again.

## The checklist has a shelf life

Every lead qualification checklist asks the same handful of things.
Budget, authority, need, timeline — MEDDIC's letters dressed up
differently.

The questions are fine. The problem is when you ask them.

Most checklists run once, at intake. The deal keeps moving for weeks
after that. Nothing on the original checklist is built to notice.

## Five items, and what quietly changes them

| Checklist item | What it looked like at intake | What silently breaks it |
|---|---|---|
| Budget confirmed | Champion said the money exists | Budget cycle resets; a new VP re-scopes spend |
| Economic buyer named | A name the champion mentioned | That person changes role or leaves |
| Pain has a timeline | "We need this by Q3" | The forcing event gets solved another way |
| Champion has spent effort | Set up the first call, wrote a doc | Goes quiet, stops replying to internal asks |
| Decision criteria stated | Written down before the pitch | Rewritten after a competitor's demo |

Each item was true once. That's what qualified the lead.

None of them come with an expiration date printed on the checklist.
You have to put one there yourself.

## Why nobody re-checks

Re-running the checklist risks a bad answer. A bad answer risks a
downgrade. A downgrade is one fewer deal on your board this quarter.

So the checklist gets filled in once, while the news is still good,
and left alone.

[B2B lead qualification](/blog/b2b-lead-qualification) covers the same
incentive problem at intake — a checklist filled in by whoever's
pipeline number depends on the answer. This is that same problem, one
month later, with nobody watching.

## Two dates, not one

Pick two points and put them on the deal, not just the checklist.

First: intake, same as always. Second: the week before the deal goes
into a forecast call — the moment a stale "yes" turns into a number
someone else has to defend.

A stall longer than two weeks earns a third check. Stalls are exactly
when a budget owner has quietly left or a champion has quietly gone
cold.

[Sales lead evaluation framework](/blog/sales-lead-evaluation-framework)
is the private version of this same discipline — run on yourself, before
anyone else sees the answer.

## Run the second check before someone else does

The honest re-check works best done alone, before a forecast call
turns a stale "yes" into a commitment you have to walk back later.

## Marcus, the second deal

Same five items. Same intake pass, all green.

This time he set a second date — the Friday before the forecast call —
and checked again.

Two items had gone stale. The budget owner had changed. The champion
hadn't replied in nine days.

He flagged both before the call, not during it. The deal didn't die
this time. It just took an honest conversation instead of a surprise.
