Open an inside sales dashboard and three unrelated families of number sit
on one screen.

They get read as one measurement. They aren't.

## Three kinds of metric, one screen

Each family answers a different question, and none of them answers the
other two.

| Metric family | What it counts | What it can't see |
|---|---|---|
| Activity | Dials, connects, connect rate | Whether the conversation went anywhere once it started |
| Conversation | Talk ratio, sentiment, question count | Whether the deal actually moved |
| Outcome | Next step booked, stage change | Whether that next step will hold, or why it happened |

Stack all three on a dashboard and they read like layers of the same
picture. They're closer to three separate photographs of different
rooms.

## Why activity metrics come first

Dials and connect rate exist because they're the easiest thing to count
automatically, with no human judgment anywhere in the pipeline.

They're also the least informative. A high connect rate says you reached
people. It says nothing about the thirty seconds after someone picked up
— the part where the call actually succeeds or dies.

## Why conversation metrics get treated as the fix

Talk ratio and sentiment showed up to answer the question activity
metrics can't: what happened during the call, not just whether it
happened at all.

[Sales call transcript analysis metrics](/blog/sales-call-transcript-analysis-metrics)
covers what those four numbers are actually built from. They're
computable straight off a transcript, which is exactly why they scaled
across inside sales teams so fast.

They still don't reach the outcome. A rep can hit a clean ratio and a
warm sentiment score on a call that goes nowhere.
[Gong scorecards](/blog/gong-scorecards) turned that specific gap into a
standing complaint among reps who watch their number move without their
pipeline moving with it.

## Why outcome metrics aren't the answer either

A booked next step looks like proof the call worked.

Sometimes it's proof the buyer wanted to end the call politely. Stage
changes and booked meetings measure what happened right after — not
whether the rep left with a better read on the deal, or a worse one.
[Sales call performance](/blog/sales-call-performance) makes the case
for that distinction at length.

## The question none of the three ask

Every family on the dashboard measures something that happened to the
call — how often, how it was conducted, what got scheduled next.

None of them ask what the rep now knows that they didn't know before
the call started. [AI call analysis](/blog/ai-call-analysis) is built
around that gap: the read only exists in the rep's head, and only for
about twenty minutes before it turns into a summary for someone else.
