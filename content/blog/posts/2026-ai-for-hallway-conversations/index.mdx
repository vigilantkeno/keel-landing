The meeting ends at 2:58. The ops lead walks you to the elevator and,
thirty seconds from the door, says the thing she didn't want to say in
front of her VP.

No agenda got that out of her. Distance from the room did.

## The meeting is not where people are most honest

Every sales process is built around the room — the call, the demo, the
deck. It's also, structurally, the place people are least candid.

Someone's boss is on the call. A whole team is watching how the
stakeholder plays it. The room has an audience, and audiences change
what gets said.

The hallway doesn't have one. That's not a coincidence. It's the whole
reason the comment that matters tends to land there instead.

## Hallway conversations don't fit any tool built for meetings

Notetakers need a calendar invite to join. Recorders need a dial-in to
attach to. The entire sales-AI category assumes something was
scheduled first.

A hallway conversation was never scheduled. It has no invite, no
dial-in, no event for a tool to find.

So it goes uncaptured — not because it doesn't matter, but because
nothing in the stack was built to notice it happening.

## Where these conversations actually show up

Being walked out after a meeting, thirty seconds to the elevator.

A stakeholder catching you by the coffee machine with a question they
didn't ask in the room.

The lunch before a demo, where someone mentions who else is in the
room for the real decision.

A conference hallway between two booth conversations, said to no one
in particular except you.

None of it looks like a meeting. All of it is deal-relevant, and most
of it is gone by the time you're back at your desk.

## What actually needs to happen with it

The instinct is to remember it later. Don't.

Say it out loud the moment you're clear of the hallway — walking to
your car, riding the elevator down, before the next thing starts
competing for the same three details.

You're not writing a report. You're doing what you'd do telling a
colleague what just happened: naming who said it, what changed, and
why it mattered.

That's the whole method. It works because it happens before the
detail fades, not because it's elaborate.

## Why writing it down later doesn't work

By the time you're at your desk, the hallway comment has already
started blurring into "she seemed positive." The specific words are
gone. What's left is a vibe, and a vibe doesn't help you three weeks
from now when you're deciding how to play the next call.

[The same problem shows up everywhere a deal moves off the
calendar](/blog/ai-for-in-between-moments) — the drive home, the
lunch, the coffee machine. The hallway is just the sharpest version of
it, because it happens right after the room, while the contrast
between what got said inside and what got said outside is still
obvious.

A [drive-home debrief](/blog/drive-home-from-sales-meeting) captures
the whole meeting. A hallway capture is narrower and faster — one
comment, thirty seconds, said before the elevator doors close.

## The tool has to work without being asked to open first

A rep with ninety seconds doesn't have time for a login screen or a
project picker. If capturing the moment takes longer than the moment
lasted, it doesn't happen.

So it has to work like a phone call. Talk, and it's held — no folder
to choose, no tagging, nothing to configure standing in a hallway.

That's the actual design bar. Not smarter transcription. Just nothing
standing between the rep and saying the thing before it's gone.
