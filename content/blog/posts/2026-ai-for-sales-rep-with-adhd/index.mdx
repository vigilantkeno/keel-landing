You walk out of a great call. The prospect said something real about their
budget cycle, you caught it, you knew in the moment it mattered. Then your
phone buzzes with the next meeting starting in four minutes, your brain
snaps forward to that one, and by the time you sit down to update the CRM
that night, what's left is "good call, follow up next week." The detail
that actually mattered is gone. Not because you didn't care. Because your
attention had already moved on before you had a chance to write it down.

## The tools assume a brain that isn't yours

Every CRM, every pipeline tracker, every "just log it after the call"
process was built around a specific kind of user: someone who can hold a
thought, sit down, and transcribe it faithfully into a field, consistently,
call after call, day after day. That's not a neutral design choice. It's an
assumption about how attention and follow-through work — and for a lot of
reps, it's just not how their attention works.

If you've ever finished a call knowing exactly what mattered and lost it
by the time you had a spare minute to write it down, that's not a
discipline problem. It's a gap between when the insight is live and when
the system finally asks for it. For an ADHD brain, that gap is where
everything falls out.

## The job is actually a good fit. The tooling is the mismatch.

Here's the part that gets missed: sales is one of the better-suited careers
for an ADHD brain, not a worse one. The pace, the variety, the live
problem-solving, the reward that shows up fast instead of six months later
— reps with ADHD often outperform in the room. The hard part isn't selling.
It's everything that's supposed to happen around the selling: the
between-calls admin that has no urgency, no stakes, no immediate feedback,
and every reason to get bumped by whatever's more interesting right now.

That's not a motivation gap. Tasks with a deadline three days out and zero
immediate consequence are exactly the category ADHD brains deprioritize —
not from carelessness, but because the brain's alarm system runs on
urgency, and "update the deal stage" doesn't trigger one.

## What breaks first: the stuff with no immediate feedback

Watch where the pipeline actually decays and it's never the calls
themselves — it's the connective tissue between them. The follow-up you
meant to send the same day and sent four days later, once the moment had
cooled. The CRM note that says "discussed pricing" instead of what was
actually said about pricing. The three open threads with different
stakeholders that all needed a reply and only one got one, because it was
the one still on screen when the next fire started.

None of that is about the deal being hard. It's about the gap between
"I know what I should do" and "I did it before the window closed" — and for
an ADHD brain, that gap is wider and closes faster than most tools account
for.

## Why "just make a task list" doesn't fix it

The advice every ADHD rep has already heard: make a list, set a reminder,
block time on the calendar. It's not wrong, and it's also not enough,
because the list itself is a task with no immediate reward — which means
it competes for the same attention it's supposed to organize, and often
loses.

The actual failure point isn't remembering that you need to follow up. Most
reps with ADHD know that. It's the gap between the call ending and the
follow-up existing anywhere outside your head — and a to-do app doesn't
close that gap, because you still have to sit down, remember what mattered,
and type it in before the next thing arrives.

## What actually works: capture at the moment it happens, not after

The fix that holds up isn't a better list. It's moving the capture point to
the second the information is still live — right after the call, not that
night, not "when I get a minute." Talk it through out loud for ninety
seconds while it's still fresh: what they said, what surprised you, what
you need to do next. That's the entire trick. Not more discipline.
A shorter gap between the thought and the record of it.

This matches how ADHD attention actually behaves, instead of fighting it.
You're not trying to hold five open threads in working memory until end of
day. You're offloading each one within minutes of it forming, before the
next thing arrives to bump it out.

## What a week looks like when the capture point moves

You finish a call and, walking to your car, you talk through what just
happened instead of letting it evaporate — what the buyer pushed back on,
who else needs to be looped in, the thing they said about next quarter that
you'd have forgotten by lunch. Thirty seconds after your next meeting ends,
you do it again, before the day's momentum carries you past it.

By Friday, instead of trying to reconstruct a week of half-remembered
detail into a forecast update, you're pulling from a running record of what
actually happened, call by call, captured in the only window when capturing
it was ever realistic — right after, while it was still there.

Nothing about this requires a new discipline you don't already have. It
requires a capture point that doesn't depend on remembering to use it
later.
