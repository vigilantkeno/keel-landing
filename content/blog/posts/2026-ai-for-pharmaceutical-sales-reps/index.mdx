Elena gets four minutes with Dr. Okafor between patients, standing in the
hallway outside exam room three. She mentions the new indication data, he
asks one pointed question about a drug interaction, and then he's gone —
back in with his next patient before she's finished her sentence. She has
until end of day to log the call, accurately, in the CRM her compliance
team audits.

## Pharma reps already live inside more documentation than almost anyone

It's a common assumption that pharma sales is under-instrumented compared
to other B2B motions. The opposite is true. Every provider interaction
generates a call note — what was discussed, what samples or materials were
left, what the prescriber said — logged for Sunshine Act reporting,
internal audit, and promotional-conduct review. Pharma reps have been
documenting their calls in detail for longer than "sales AI" has existed
as a product category.

So the problem was never a lack of documentation. It's that the
documentation happens the hard way: from memory, hours after a hallway
conversation that lasted four minutes, competing with the six other
provider visits that happened the same day.

## Recording the visit doesn't solve this — it adds a second compliance problem

The instinct from a generic sales-AI vendor is to solve documentation with
a recorder: attach to the visit, capture the audio, generate the note
automatically. That instinct breaks immediately in this vertical. A
third-party tool capturing audio of a conversation with a prescriber, in
or near a clinical space, is exactly the kind of uncontrolled data capture
that hospital and practice compliance offices exist to prevent — on top
of the promotional-conduct scrutiny pharma sales already operates under.
[We've written about why this fails structurally](/blog/sales-ai-for-regulated-industries)
across regulated verticals generally; pharma is one of the sharpest cases
of it, because the documentation requirement already exists and doesn't
need a recording to satisfy it.

The rep doesn't need a tool that captures the conversation. She needs a
tool that captures her account of the conversation, fast, before the
next hallway and the next four minutes erase the details of this one.

## The real bottleneck is the gap between the visit and the note

Ask any pharma rep where accuracy actually breaks down and it's rarely
the visit itself — it's the six or eight hours between the visit and
whenever there's finally time to sit down and write the call note. By
then, Dr. Okafor's specific question about drug interactions has started
blurring into the question a different prescriber asked that morning.

That gap is where detail gets lost or, worse, gets reconstructed
generically — a call note that says "discussed efficacy data, prescriber
receptive" because the specific exchange didn't survive the day. A
compliance function reading that note six months later can't tell the
difference between a rep who remembered precisely and one who filled in a
template.

## What actually helps: capture in the elevator, not at the desk

The fix isn't a better form to fill out at day's end. It's closing the
gap between the hallway and the record — talking through what just
happened while walking to the car, before the next office, before the
detail fades. That's the same logic behind
[in-person sales meeting AI](/blog/in-person-sales-meeting-ai) generally:
the tool's job is the moments around the visit, not the visit itself,
because nothing should be capturing the visit itself.

For a pharma rep, that means being able to talk through a visit by voice
between offices — what the prescriber asked, what got left behind, what
follow-up was promised — and have that held accurately until it's time to
turn it into the compliance-required note. The rep still writes the
record. The tool just stops the four-minute conversation from evaporating
before she gets the chance to.

Pharma sales was never missing a compliance process — it has one of the
