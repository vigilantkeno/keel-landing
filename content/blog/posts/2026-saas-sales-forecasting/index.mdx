A SaaS forecast usually has three kinds of revenue on one line: new,
expansion, renewal.

Only one of them is a deal that closes or doesn't, on a date.

The other two get forecast the same way anyway, and that's most of
where the number goes wrong.

## The math everyone reaches for

Ask what makes SaaS forecasting different and you'll hear about
cohort-based models, net-revenue-retention adjustments, usage-weighted
scoring.

All real techniques. All aimed at the aggregate — [a better formula
applied across a larger sample of accounts](/blog/sales-forecasting-techniques).

None of them ask where the number for one specific account's expansion
actually came from.

## New business is a different kind of event

A new-logo deal has a shape: discovery, a proposal, a decision, a
close date. Rounded up or not, [it's at least the same kind of event
every time](/blog/sales-forecasting-vs-pipeline-management).

Expansion and renewal don't work that way.

Usage climbs or drops over weeks. A champion goes quiet, or a new VP
shows up with different priorities. There's no single call where an
account "decides" to expand. There's a slow accumulation of signal an
AE or CS rep is reading the whole time — and writing down almost none
of it.

## What blending them hides

Put a new deal, a renewal, and an expansion opportunity in the same
weighted-pipeline line and the forecast can't tell you which part is
solid.

The new deal has a real stage behind its number. [The renewal's number
is often just last year's contract value, carried forward out of
habit](/blog/pipeline-forecasting) — not a read on whether the account
is actually healthy.

One line, three different levels of confidence, reported as if they
were the same kind of fact.

## Where the actual read lives

Ask a CS or AE who owns an expansion account how it's going and
they'll usually answer in a sentence, not a percentage.

"Usage is up, but the champion who'd sign off just left." That
sentence is the real forecast input. It's also the thing that never
makes it into a stage field, because the field wasn't built for
accounts — it was built for deals with a close date.

[The same gap a forecast-by-rep report can't
close](/blog/forecast-by-rep): the number gets flagged as off, and by
then the sentence behind it is three weeks stale.

## What actually fixes it

Not a better blended model. A separate, honest line for the accounts
that aren't closing on a date — captured as a sentence, when the read
changes, not reconstructed at renewal-quarter panic.

"I'd call this a real 40%, not the 80% carried forward from last
year's contract" is worth more to a SaaS forecast than any weighting
scheme applied to a number nobody actually believes.

## Three lines, not one

New business, expansion, and renewal were never the same forecasting
problem.

Treating them as one blended number doesn't make SaaS forecasting more
sophisticated. It just spreads one deal's real confidence across two
accounts that never earned it.
