By hour four on the floor, the badge scanner has a rhythm. Scan, talk,
scan, talk.

By hour eight, the pocket has forty slips of paper and the memory has
maybe twelve distinct conversations left in it.

The flight home doesn't get that memory back.

## The speed advice solves the wrong problem

Every conference playbook says the same thing: follow up within
24 to 48 hours, before the prospect forgets you.

That advice assumes the rep is the one who'll remember. Usually
neither person does — the prospect had a dozen booth conversations too,
and the rep had forty.

## What actually happens between the booth and the inbox

Conversation eight blurs into conversation nine somewhere around lunch.
By the last session of day two, the specific thing that made lead
twenty-three worth a follow-up is gone.

What survives is a badge scan and a job title. Everything that made
the conversation a lead instead of a scan — the actual pain they
named, the competitor they mentioned, the "call me in Q1" they said
with a straight face — is the part that doesn't survive.

## Why the fast, generic email underperforms

A same-day email sent to two hundred scanned badges reads exactly like
what it is. The prospect can tell in one sentence whether the sender
remembers them specifically or is working through a list.

Speed without specificity doesn't build the relationship the
conversation started. It just closes the loop faster on a version of
the conversation that never actually happened.

A slower email that opens with the actual detail — the tool they said
they were evaluating, the timeline they mentioned — reads as a real
person who was there. That's the email that gets a reply.

## The fix has to happen at the booth, not after the flight

The detail that makes a follow-up land has to get captured in the
gap between conversations, not reconstructed from memory three days
later.

That gap is short. A few steps to the next aisle, maybe a minute
before the next person walks up.

It's just long enough to talk through what the last conversation
actually covered — out loud, the way you'd tell a colleague walking
next to you — if there's something there to catch it.

## What a real follow-up needs to reference

Not the company name and title. Those are already in the scan.

The thing that makes a prospect feel remembered is the detail nobody
else at the booth would have said the same way: the specific problem,
the specific timeline, the joke they made about their own procurement
process.

That's what turns "thanks for stopping by" into a conversation the
prospect actually continues.
