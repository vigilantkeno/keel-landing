"What went well. What didn't."

That's the reflection template most reps already know by heart. And
it's the reason most post-call reflection changes nothing.

## The questions are written for the wrong reader

"What went well" is a status-update question. It assumes someone
might read the answer — a manager, a CRM field, a future version of
you preparing to explain the call to someone else.

That assumption changes the answer before you've written a word of
it. You reach for the version of the call that holds up, not the one
that's true.

Ask a rep "what went well" right after a tough call and watch how
fast the answer turns into a defense.

## A private debrief asks the question you'd censor

Nobody polices the drive home. Nobody's grading the ten seconds
after you hang up. That's exactly what makes it useful — if you let
it be.

Most reps still fill that private moment with the same safe question
a manager would ask, because it's the only template they've got.

The honest version asks something narrower: what did you actually
notice, not what would sound reasonable if repeated back to someone.

## Three questions that only work unaudienced

**What did I avoid saying?** Not what you said — what you didn't,
because it would've slowed the call down or made things
uncomfortable.

**What am I hoping is true that I don't actually know?** "They're
excited" and "they said they're excited" are different sentences.
Most reps blur them by the second follow-up.

**What would I tell a friend who described this exact call to me?**
You'd probably be blunter with a friend's deal than your own. That
gap is the tell.

None of these have a dropdown answer. That's not a flaw in the
method — it's what makes the answer worth having.

## Why this beats a template

A template optimizes for completeness — every field filled, nothing
skipped. A private debrief optimizes for the one thing you'd
otherwise let yourself forget you noticed.

The meeting is the performance. The reflection right after is the
work — and work that assumes a grader stops being reflection at all.

It becomes a second performance, quieter than the first, but still a
performance.

## This is a different scale than a full deal review

[A structured deal review](/blog/deal-reflection-framework) walks
the whole account — every stage, every stakeholder, weeks of
history.

This is smaller on purpose. One call, three questions, a few
minutes. It's the version you can actually do every time, not just
before the deal review that matters.

Doing the small version consistently beats doing the big version
occasionally. Most deals aren't lost in the review. They're lost in
the calls nobody looked at honestly in between.
