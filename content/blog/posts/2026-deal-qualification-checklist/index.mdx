Owen runs the checklist on a new deal. Budget, buyer, timeline,
champion, criteria.

Five boxes. Five checks. The deal moves to the next stage.

Nothing on the sheet says how solid any of those five checks actually
are.

## A checklist only records yes or no

That's the whole design. Ask a question, get an answer, check a box.

It's a fast tool for a good reason. A rep on a call doesn't have time
to write an essay under "budget confirmed."

But the box only holds one bit of information. Checked, or not.

## Two very different yeses

Deal one: "budget confirmed," because the champion forwarded a signed
PO.

Deal two: "budget confirmed," because the champion said "yeah, we've
got budget for this" on a call.

Same checkbox. Same checkmark. Same five green boxes on the sheet by
the time the deal reaches a forecast call.

One of those two boxes is load-bearing. The other is a guess wearing a
checkmark.

## What the checkmark erases

[MEDDIC questions](/blog/meddic-questions) makes a related point about
a single field — every letter has a fast version that fills the box
and a slower version that actually tests the claim. This is what
happens after either version gets asked: the checklist can't tell you
which route a given box took to get checked.

[Sales qualification process](/blog/sales-qualification-process) covers
a different failure in the same family — the checklist only runs once.
[Lead qualification checklist](/blog/lead-qualification-checklist)
covers a third — the checked answer goes stale over the weeks after
intake. Neither problem needs a shaky answer. This one only shows up
when the answer was never that strong to begin with.

## Ranking the boxes instead of counting them

Five checks isn't the same as five confirmations.

Ask, for each checked box: who told you this, and what did it cost
them to say it. A signed PO cost someone a signature. A champion's
verbal reassurance cost nothing.

Rank the five from most load-bearing to least. The rank tells you
something the checkmark never could — which one box, if it turns out
to be wrong, actually sinks the deal.
