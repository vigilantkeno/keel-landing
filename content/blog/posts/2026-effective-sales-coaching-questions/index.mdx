"Did you ask about budget?"

"What made you think budget wasn't the real blocker?"

Both are sales coaching questions. Only one of them teaches anything.

## The question that tests recall

Most coaching questions check whether a step happened.

Did you ask about timeline. Did you get to the decision-maker. Did you
handle the objection the way the training said to.

Useful for a rep's first month — there's a real process to learn, and
recall questions confirm it landed.

Past that stretch, they stop teaching much. A rep can answer every
recall question correctly and still misread the deal completely.

## The question that tests judgment

Effective coaching questions ask what the rep believed, not what they
did.

What made you think the champion was actually bought in. What would
have changed your read if the buyer had hesitated half a second longer.
What are you willing to bet the real blocker is, and why.

Those questions have no scripted right answer. That's what makes them
work — they surface the reasoning, not the compliance.

## A three-question checklist

Skip the twenty-item rubric. Three questions, asked in order, do more.

- **What actually happened?** Not the summary you'd give your manager —
  the version with the parts that don't flatter you left in.
- **What did you believe going in that changed?** The moment your
  certainty about the deal shifted, and what caused it.
- **What would you do differently next time?** Specific to this deal,
  not a general resolution.

Ask them out of order and the exercise collapses back into a status
report. The third question only means something once the first two
happened honestly.

[Deal coaching](/blog/deal-coaching) covers four same-day questions
built for one specific deal. This checklist is the shape those
questions should take, whichever deal they're about.

## Why the order matters more than the wording

A rep who jumps straight to "what would you do differently" is
guessing at a lesson without doing the diagnostic work first.

The guess usually sounds reasonable. It's rarely the actual mistake.
[Sales coaching app](/blog/sales-coaching-app) makes the related case
about timing — the honest version of what happened has a short shelf
life, and a rushed checklist run days later gets the sequence right but
the substance wrong.
