You sent the recap Tuesday. Nothing. You sent "just checking in" Friday.
Nothing. You sent it again the following Wednesday with slightly different
wording, the way you'd rephrase a question for someone who might not have
understood it the first time. Still nothing. Three weeks in, you've started
describing a real person with a real budget and a real reason for talking to
you in the first place as someone who "went dark" — like they're a satellite
that lost signal, not a buyer who made a decision you weren't in the room
for.

## "Ghosted" is a word for strangers. This isn't that.

Ghosting, as a term, comes from dating — a way to describe someone who
disappears with zero explanation because they owe you none. That's not what
happened in your pipeline. This person took a call with you. Maybe three. They
asked real questions. Somewhere between the last call and now, something
shifted — and the silence you're getting isn't nothing, it's information you
haven't decoded yet.

Calling it ghosting lets you stop there. It's a word that ends the
investigation before it starts, because "they ghosted me" sounds like a fact
about them, not a gap in what you know. It's rarely a fact about them.

## What's actually happening when a deal goes quiet

Run through what silence usually means, once you stop treating it as a
verdict:

Priorities moved. Something more urgent landed on their desk — not about
you, not a rejection of you, just a queue that reordered itself without
sending you a memo.

Budget got harder. Someone above them said something in a meeting you
weren't invited to, and now they need an internal answer before they can
give you one.

An objection never got said out loud. Your champion had a concern three
calls ago that they hedged instead of stated plainly, and it's still sitting
there, unaddressed, quietly deciding the outcome.

They're avoiding a conversation, not a person. Telling you "we're going
with someone else" or "we're pausing this" takes an act of will most people
don't have on a Tuesday. Silence costs them nothing. A real answer costs
them an uncomfortable ten minutes.

None of these are about your last email being bad. All of them are about
something happening on their side of the table that you can't see from
yours — which means the fix isn't a better-written follow-up. It's
figuring out which of these you're actually dealing with.

## The real problem isn't the silence. It's what you don't remember.

Here's the part that makes a quiet deal so much harder to work than it
should be: by the time you notice someone's gone dark, the moment that
explains it is already three calls back. The hesitation on the pricing
question. The stakeholder they mentioned once and never again. The half-joke
about "budget conversations upstairs" that you let pass because the meeting
moved on.

None of that made it into a CRM field, because none of it looked important
enough to log in the moment — it just sounded like a person talking. It's
only in hindsight, staring at an unanswered email, that it becomes the
answer you needed three weeks ago.

This is the actual shape of the problem: the deal didn't go dark suddenly.
It went dark the way things always do — a series of small moments, each one
easy to miss, adding up to a customer who stopped responding. You just
didn't have anywhere to put those moments while they were still small enough
to notice.

## The reflex that makes it worse

The instinct, when someone stops responding, is to follow up again. And
again. Each one restates the pitch, a little more politely worded than the
last, a little more obviously anxious underneath. We've written before about
[how to follow up without turning into the person a prospect dreads
hearing from](/blog/how-to-follow-up-without-being-annoying) — the short
version is that repetition reads as pressure, and pressure is exactly the
wrong signal to send someone who's already avoiding a hard conversation
with you.

A fourth "just checking in" doesn't make the silence more likely to break.
It usually does the opposite: it turns a stalled deal into an actively
avoided one, because now responding to you means responding to the
follow-ups too.

## What to actually do instead

Stop sending the same message reworded. Go back through what you actually
know — not what's in the CRM, what you remember saying to each other, on
every call — and find the specific thing you can reference that isn't "just
checking in." The hesitation about implementation timeline. The
half-mentioned budget conversation. The stakeholder who came up once and
never again.

Then send one message built entirely around that thing. Not "wanted to
follow up" — "you mentioned the finance conversation was still open, how did
that land?" Specificity is the entire difference between a message that gets
answered and one that gets archived. A generic check-in asks them to do
work — remember why this mattered, re-engage from zero. A specific one
proves you were actually listening, which is rarer than it should be and
noticed every time.

If you genuinely can't reconstruct the specific thread — if the calls have
blurred into a general sense that things were going fine until they weren't
— that's the real finding here. Not that the customer is unresponsive. That
you don't have a record of the moments that would tell you why.

## Build the habit before the silence, not after

The fix isn't a better template for the fifth follow-up. It's capturing what
actually gets said on the second and third calls, while it's still fresh,
before it becomes the vague "I think they mentioned something about budget"
you're trying to reconstruct three weeks later. A five-minute debrief after
a call — what they said, what they hesitated on, who else came up — turns
into the exact ammunition you need the moment things go quiet.

Most reps don't skip this because they don't see the value. They skip it
because doing it by hand, after every call, on top of everything else a
pipeline demands, doesn't survive a real week. The habit needs to be easier
than the alternative, or it doesn't happen.
