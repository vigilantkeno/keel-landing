Ask a sales rep why they're leaving and they'll say comp.

Ask again in six months, off the record, and you'll usually hear a
different answer.

## The exit interview always says the same thing

Comp is the safe answer. It's specific, it's uncontestable, and it
doesn't require anyone in the room to admit what actually happened.

Nobody gets defensive about a number. Everybody gets defensive about
"I stopped trusting my own read on my own deals."

So comp is what goes on the form, and the org fixes comp, and the next
quarter someone else leaves for "comp" too.

## What comp usually stands in for

Three things, almost always tangled together under one word.

**No ceiling.** Not a title problem — a growth problem. A rep who can't
see what year three looks like starts treating year one like the whole
career.

**No say in how the work gets done.** Every call scripted, every metric
tracked, every deviation flagged. The job stops feeling like selling
and starts feeling like compliance with a quota attached.

**No visible line from effort to outcome.** A rep who closes a hard deal
and can't point to what they actually did differently has no way to
repeat it — or to prove it wasn't luck.

Any one of those is survivable. All three, and comp becomes the
convenient word for something much harder to say out loud.

## The version that's accelerating

There's a sharper edge on the second one lately: being scored on
activity instead of judgment. [Rep quitting over AI
monitoring](/blog/rep-quitting-over-ai-monitoring) is the pointed
version of this — call scoring and monitoring tools that convert
selling into a performance for whoever's watching the dashboard.

That's not the only driver of burnout. But it's the one making an old
problem measurable, loud, and suddenly urgent enough that reps bring
it up unprompted.

A rep who hits every number and still gets told the read "doesn't feel
right" learns fast that the job isn't closing deals anymore. It's
narrating them convincingly.

## Longevity looks different from what gets rewarded

The reps who stay five years rarely describe lighter quotas as the
reason.

They describe control over how they work, and a clear line from what
they did to what happened next. [Trust-based
selling](/blog/trust-based-selling) is what an org looks like when it's
actually built for that — judgment instead of a scorecard, on purpose.

Take the judgment away and even a rep who's winning starts
job-hunting quietly, long before comp ever comes up.

Comp will always be the answer on the form. It's the one nobody has to
defend.
