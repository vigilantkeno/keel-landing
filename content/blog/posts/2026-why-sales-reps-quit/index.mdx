Marcus's resignation letter says "new opportunity." His manager didn't
push back, because the new offer really was better.

That's not why he left. He'd been looking for six months before the
offer ever showed up.

## What every exit interview says

Ask why a rep left and you'll hear one of three answers. Comp fell
behind market. The territory got cut. The manager was difficult.

All three show up constantly, and all three are usually true as far as
they go.

None of them explains why the search started before there was anything
to leave for.

## The pattern underneath the stated reasons

Comp, territory, and a bad manager have one thing in common: they're
all situations where the rep stopped feeling trusted.

A cut territory reads as "we don't trust your judgment about where to
spend your time." A micromanaging boss reads as "we don't trust you to
run your own calls." Behind-market comp reads as "we don't trust you
enough to pay for what you're worth."

The trigger is different every time. The mechanism underneath is the
same — a slow accumulation of moments where a rep's own read on a deal
stopped being the one that counted.

## The part nobody puts in the exit interview

Nobody writes "I got tired of managing how my calls would look" on a
resignation letter. It sounds like an excuse, not a reason.

Watch behavior instead. Reps who are already halfway out start keeping
their honest read of a deal somewhere nobody official can see it — a
notes app, a text to themselves, the version they only say out loud to
a peer.

That's not a discipline problem. It's a rep protecting the one place
their judgment still counts, once the sanctioned system stopped feeling
safe for the honest version.

## Why this is the burnout, not just the trigger

Sales rep burnout rarely looks like exhaustion from too many calls.

It looks like a rep who's started doing two jobs on every conversation
— selling, and managing how the selling will read to someone reviewing
it later.

That second job is the one that actually wears people out. [Rep quitting over AI monitoring](/blog/rep-quitting-over-ai-monitoring)
goes deeper into the specific mechanism when the second job is a
scoring tool. This is the wider pattern: any structure that makes a rep
perform their own judgment, instead of just using it, produces the same
slow drain.

## What a sustainable sales career actually protects

A career that lasts isn't the one with the fewest hard quarters. It's
the one where a rep never fully lost the habit of trusting their own
read.

That habit needs somewhere private to keep running — a place to say
"that call went worse than I'm about to admit" without it becoming a
data point in a review. Reps who have that somewhere hold onto judgment
years longer than reps whose every call gets graded the moment it ends.

Marcus's manager will tell the next VP that comp was the issue, and
technically it was. It just wasn't the whole six months.
