Most discovery-question lists read the same. What's your timeline.
What's your budget. Who else is involved.

Every one of those has a comfortable, rehearsed answer. A prospect
can say all three without giving anything away.

A discovery question is only doing its job when the honest answer
costs the prospect something to produce.

## What makes a question comfortable

A comfortable question has an answer the prospect already carries
around, unchanged, for every vendor who's ever asked it.

"What's your timeline" gets the same reply whether the deal is real
or imaginary — "end of quarter," "we're evaluating now." It costs
nothing to say and confirms nothing.

[The same failure shows up on qualification
scorecards](/blog/sales-lead-evaluation-framework): a field filled in
generously, because filling it in costs the rep nothing either.

## The four that actually cost something

**"What have you already tried?"** A prospect with a real problem has
scar tissue — a tool that didn't work, a process someone built and
quietly abandoned. Silence here, or a vague "we've looked at a few
things," is the tell.

**"Who signs, and have you talked to them yet?"** Not "who's the
decision maker" — that gets a title. This gets a name, or an
admission that the conversation hasn't happened.

**"What happens if you do nothing?"** A prospect who's actually
thought it through has a specific, uncomfortable answer. One who
hasn't reaches for something vague, like "it'll keep being a
problem."

**"Why now, instead of six months ago?"** [Urgency a rep introduced
is not urgency the buyer already had](/blog/b2b-lead-qualification).
This question separates the two — a manufactured reason sounds
different out loud than a real one does.

## Why the awkward pause is the signal

A good discovery question should occasionally produce a pause, not a
smooth answer.

That pause is the prospect actually retrieving the truth instead of
reciting a rehearsed line.

[Reps trained to fill every silence miss it](/blog/deal-coaching).
The silence was the answer arriving, not a gap to be polite about.

If every question in the call gets an instant, confident reply, the
questions were already answered before the call started.

## Ask fewer, and let them land

A long discovery checklist invites a prospect onto autopilot,
question twelve blurring into question thirteen.

Four questions that actually cost something to answer beat fifteen
that don't. The prospect notices the difference, even without being
able to say why the call felt different.
