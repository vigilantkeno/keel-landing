Your pipeline has a folder for deals like this — quiet, not closed,
sitting right where they stalled three weeks ago.

Most reps run the same play on every one of them: another check-in,
another week of silence, repeat. That treats every quiet deal like
it's asleep. Some of them are already dead.

## Two things look identical from your side of the table

A dormant deal and a dead one send you the exact same signal:
nothing. No reply, no read receipt, no reason given.

The silence itself doesn't tell you which one you're looking at.

That's the actual problem with a quiet deal. It's not that nobody's
answering — it's that you're guessing which kind of nobody you're
dealing with, and the guess decides how you spend the next month.

## What actually separates dormant from dead

Three things matter here, and length of silence isn't one of them.

The first is money. A dormant deal has a real budget conversation
behind it — a number, a process, a timeline someone said out loud.
A dead one has "we're exploring options" and nothing more specific,
ever.

The second is the champion — someone who sat through two or three
calls and pushed the deal internally is worth chasing, and someone
you talked to once who never resurfaces even in cc was never going
to fight for this.

The third is the last real signal. Positive-but-slow is dormant. A
soft "let me think about it" you reframed as interest, because the
alternative was admitting a no, is dead — you just haven't logged it
as one yet.

## Dormant vs. dead

| | Dormant | Dead |
|---|---|---|
| Budget conversation | A number, a timeline | Never got specific |
| Champion | Engaged across multiple calls | One call, then gone |
| Last live signal | Positive but slow | A soft no you reframed |
| What the silence means | A queue that reordered | A decision already made |
| Right move | One sharp, specific message | Close it and move on |

The left column is worth your next hour. The right column is worth
accepting.

## The wake-up move that isn't another check-in

A dormant deal doesn't need a nudge. It needs a reason — something
that changed, that they'd actually want to know about, that isn't
"just following up."

Referencing the old pitch again doesn't work, because the old pitch
isn't what went quiet. Something else did, and you don't know what
yet.

So don't lead with the pitch. Lead with a real update: a relevant
change on your side, a story that maps to the exact objection they
never quite said out loud, anything that gives them a reason to open
the message that isn't guilt.

One line. One reason. One clear next step — not a recap of
everything you've already told them.

## Why the fifth "just checking in" kills it faster

Every unanswered check-in doesn't sit there neutrally, waiting for a
reply. It compounds.

By message four or five, responding to you means acknowledging three
that went unanswered — a more awkward moment than staying quiet ever
was. You've made silence the easier option.

A single specific message breaks that pattern. It isn't asking them
to explain three weeks of nothing. It's giving them one new thing to
react to.

## When to actually let it go

If the triage comes back dead — no real budget conversation, a
champion who never came back after one call, a "let me think about
it" that was really a no — stop.

Closing it in your pipeline isn't giving up. It's admitting a
decision already got made without you in the room, and it frees up
the hours you were about to spend proving you don't know that yet.

The dead deals aren't the problem. The problem is treating every
quiet deal like it might be one of the dormant ones, forever,
because closing it feels like losing and re-pitching feels like
trying.
