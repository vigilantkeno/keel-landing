Three read receipts, then nothing. The open-rate pings stopped nine
days ago, and you've started reading that silence as a verdict.

It isn't one. Not yet.

## Cold is not dead

A dead deal has a reason attached to it — lost to a competitor,
budget killed, project shelved. Somebody said so.

A cold deal has no reason attached to anything. It just stopped
generating signal, and an absence of signal isn't the same fact as a
no.

We've written about [the other version of this](/blog/customer-ghosted)
— a customer who barely engaged in the first place. A cold deal is
the opposite problem: real engagement that stalled, which means the
fix is remembering it, not manufacturing it from nothing.

## Check three things before you send anything

**What changed on their side.** A reorg, a new VP, a shifted
quarterly priority — something moved that has nothing to do with
your pitch. Check their company's own news before you check your
inbox.

**Who else was ever in the room.** If your only contact went quiet,
that's a single-threading problem, not proof the deal died. Anyone
else who sat on a call is a second way in.

**What you last said that never got answered.** Scroll back to your
actual last message. If it was a real question, the silence might
just be an unanswered question, not a decision.

If a contact already told you a specific date — [call back next
quarter](/blog/customer-said-call-back-next-quarter) is its own kind
of cold, with its own clock — check that promise before you assume
this one needs waking up at all.

## Why "just checking in" keeps failing

A generic check-in asks the other person to do the work: remember
why this mattered, re-engage from zero, explain a silence they
haven't examined either.

Most people won't do that work for an email. They'll do it for
someone who clearly remembers the specific thing they said.

That gap — specificity — is the entire difference between a message
that gets a reply and one that gets archived.

## What actually wakes a deal up

Go back through what was actually said on the last real call, not
what's logged in the CRM, what you remember hearing. The hesitation
on the rollout timeline. The stakeholder mentioned once and never
again. The half-joke about budget that wasn't really a joke.

Reference that one thing, directly, instead of restating the pitch.
"You mentioned the finance sign-off was still open — did that land?"
gets answered. "Just following up!" gets archived.

If you can't reconstruct anything specific, that's the actual
finding here. Not that the customer went cold — that nothing from
those calls survived long enough to use.
