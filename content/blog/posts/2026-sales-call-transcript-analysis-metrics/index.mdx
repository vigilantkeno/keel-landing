The transcript comes back scored within a minute now. Talk ratio: 44%.
Longest monologue: 90 seconds. Sentiment: positive.

None of those three numbers would have told you the buyer flinched at
the renewal terms and you talked right past it.

## The four metrics every tool scores

Run a sales call transcript through almost any analysis tool and
you'll get some version of the same four numbers.

- **Talk-to-listen ratio.** How much you spoke versus the buyer, as a
  percentage. Popular because it's trivial to compute — and only
  loosely related to whether the call went well.
- **Longest monologue.** The single longest unbroken stretch you
  talked. A proxy for "did you lecture," which sometimes it catches
  and sometimes it flags a rep who was finally explaining pricing
  clearly.
- **Question count.** How many questions you asked. Rewards volume,
  not whether any question actually opened something up.
- **Sentiment or engagement score.** A model's guess at tone, scored
  from word choice and pacing. It reads confidence and evasion off the
  same cheerful words.

Every one of these is real, computable, and defensible on a
dashboard. None of them was built to answer "what should this rep do
differently next time."

## What a clean score can hide

A rep can post a textbook 40/60 talk ratio while spending the whole
call avoiding the buyer's actual objection.

A rep can blow every ratio on the sheet — talk too much, ask too few
questions, run one four-minute monologue — and still close, because
the monologue was the one honest answer the buyer needed to hear.

[Gong scorecards](/blog/gong-scorecards) turned this gap into an
entire category of rep complaints: the number moves every week, the
actual read on the deal barely changes at all.

## Why the numbers persist anyway

They're cheap to produce and easy to defend in a review. A manager can
point at 44% and 90 seconds without listening to a single minute of
the call.

That's the appeal, and it's also the limit. A metric that requires no
listening can only ever measure what a transcript makes visible — word
choice, pace, turn-taking. It was never going to reach the part of the
call that happened in the rep's head.

Tools should sharpen a rep's own judgment, not stand in as a
substitute for it. A score can flag that a call ran long. It can't
tell you whether the rep left that call more certain about the deal or
less.

## The read a transcript can't produce

Four questions, asked by the rep, immediately, do more than any scored
metric:

- Where did your certainty wobble?
- What did you dodge instead of answering?
- What surprised you?
- What would you say differently next time?

A transcript can be searched for the words that surround these
moments. It can't answer the questions themselves — only the rep who
was on the call can, and only for about twenty minutes before the
honest version fades into the story they'll tell in the pipeline
review.
