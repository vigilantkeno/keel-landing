Ana marks the deal Closed Lost on a Tuesday afternoon. The dropdown
asks for a reason, and she picks "budget," because that's the last
thing the buyer actually said out loud.

That's the version that goes in the pipeline report. It's also not
what actually happened.

## The reason field is a guess, not an analysis

Most lost deal analysis starts and ends with one dropdown, filled out
by a rep who's already moved on to the next opportunity.

The dropdown captures whatever excuse the buyer gave on the last call.
It rarely captures the moment, weeks earlier, when the deal actually
turned.

Budget is what a buyer says when they've already decided against you
and don't want to relitigate it. It's an exit line, not a diagnosis.

## A real post-mortem starts before the loss

The deal didn't die on the call where you heard "budget." It died
earlier — a stakeholder who went quiet, a demo that answered the wrong
question, a champion who stopped forwarding your emails to the
committee.

Finding that moment means walking the deal backward, not accepting the
label the buyer handed you on the way out.

Ask three questions instead of one.

When did engagement actually change, not when did the deal officially
end? Who stopped responding first, and what happened right before they
did? What did you say yes to, that in hindsight you shouldn't have?

## Why did we lose this deal — the honest version

Every deal review has an honest version and a version that's safe to
say in a pipeline meeting. The safe version blames the market, the
price, the timing.

The honest version usually names something closer to home: a
discovery call that skipped the real objection, a proposal sent before
the economic buyer was identified, a champion left to sell internally
with nothing you gave them to work with.

That version is uncomfortable. It's also the only one that changes
anything about the next deal.

## Deal autopsy, not deal excuse

An autopsy doesn't start from what the chart says. It looks at what
actually happened, in order, without deciding the answer first.

A lost deal review works the same way. Pull the timeline — every call,
every email, every gap in response time. Read it forward once, the way
it happened. Then read it backward from the loss, and find where the
thread actually broke.

The gap between those two readings is usually where the real answer
lives.

## The problem is memory, not analysis

Here's what actually makes lost deal analysis hard: by the time a deal
closes lost, most reps can't accurately reconstruct what happened three
weeks earlier.

The read you had in the room fades days after the call, long before
the deal itself is dead. That's not a discipline problem. It's what
memory does to anything that isn't written down close to the moment it
happened.

A rep who captures a private read right after every call has the raw
material for a real lost deal review. A rep who doesn't is stuck
reconstructing months of a relationship from a dropdown that says
"budget."

## What to do with what you find

A lost deal review that stops at "why we lost" wastes half its value.
The other half is pattern-matching across deals — the mistake that
keeps showing up, deal after deal, that's invisible looking at one loss
alone.

[We've written about the closest adjacent case](/blog/customer-ghosted)
— a deal that goes quiet before it's ever formally lost. The
post-mortem and the mid-deal read are the same discipline, run at
different points in the relationship.

Run the honest version enough times, and the pattern stops being a
surprise. It becomes the thing you fix before the next deal ever gets
close to needing an autopsy.
