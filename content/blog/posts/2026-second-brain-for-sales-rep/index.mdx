Somewhere between deal fourteen and deal fifteen, you stop being sure
which prospect said the budget cycle resets in January and which one said
March. Both conversations were good. Both stakeholders seemed engaged. You
have eighteen open deals and one memory, and the memory stopped scaling
around deal nine.

## The term comes from somewhere else. The problem is worse here.

"Second brain" started as a personal-productivity idea — a system outside
your head for holding what you've read, learned, and want to build on
later, so your actual brain is free to think instead of storing. It's a
good idea for a lot of jobs. It's closer to a requirement for sales,
because a rep isn't managing one long-running project. She's managing
fifteen to twenty of them at once, each with its own cast of stakeholders,
own history, own set of half-spoken concerns — and every one of them
changes on a call that happens without warning.

Knowledge workers with a second brain are usually trying to think better.
A sales rep needs one just to not lose what already happened.

## Why the details are the first thing to go

It's never the big facts that disappear — company name, deal size, close
date, all safely in the CRM. It's everything underneath that actually
decides the outcome: the exact phrase the champion used to describe her
own boss's skepticism, the offhand comment about a competing internal
project, the moment three calls ago where enthusiasm visibly cooled and
nobody named why.

None of that fits a CRM field, and none of it feels important enough to
write down in the moment — it just sounds like a person talking. It only
becomes the answer you needed once the deal stalls and you're trying to
remember what actually happened, working from a memory that's already
overwritten by the four deals you've talked to since.

## Why the generic tools don't survive contact with a real week

Reps have tried the standard second-brain stack — Notion, Obsidian, a
notes app with folders and tags — and mostly it doesn't stick. Not because
the systems are bad. Because they're built for a different rhythm: a
researcher or writer with time to sit down, think, and file something
properly, with the payoff arriving weeks or months later when the archive
proves its worth.

A rep doesn't have that rhythm. She has ninety seconds between the call
that just ended and the one that's about to start, and a system that asks
her to open an app, find the right note, choose the right tag, and write a
structured entry loses to that ninety seconds every time. The friction
doesn't have to be large. It just has to be larger than the gap, and the
gap is small.

## What a sales-specific second brain actually has to do

Strip it down to what the job actually requires and the list is short.
Capture has to take seconds, not minutes — talking it through beats
typing it out, every time, because talking is what already fits inside a
ninety-second gap. It has to organize itself around the deal without being
told to, because a rep mid-quarter isn't going to build a filing system.
And it has to surface the right memory unprompted, right before the call
where it matters — not require the rep to remember what she's forgotten
and go searching for it.

That last part is the one general tools miss worst. A note archive is only
useful if you remember to check it. The moment that actually matters is ten
minutes before a call, when you need the one detail from six weeks ago
that changes how you open the conversation — and a static notes app has no
way to hand that to you unless you already knew to look.

## What it looks like when it actually works

Ninety seconds after a call ends, you talk through what happened — what
came up, what surprised you, what you're not sure about — the way you'd
tell a colleague, not the way you'd write a report. That's the entire
capture step. No app to open, no field to fill, no filing system to
maintain.

Ten minutes before the next call with that same account, you ask what you
flagged last time, and it hands back the exact thread — the champion's
hesitation, the name of the stakeholder who came up once and never again —
instead of you trying to reconstruct it from a CRM note that says "good
call, follow up."

By the time you're managing twenty deals instead of eight, the system
hasn't gotten harder to use. It's gotten more valuable, because that's
exactly where a human memory starts failing and a captured one doesn't.
