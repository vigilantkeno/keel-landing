Your manager hands you a coaching plan template in week one.

Skill categories down the left. A 1-10 rating next to each. A review date at the bottom.

You fill it out once. Nobody opens it again until the next review cycle.

## What the template actually tracks

Discovery. Objection handling. Closing technique.

Five or six categories, scored once a quarter.

None of them point at a deal. They score you, not the pipeline you're actually running this week.

## The mismatch nobody names

Your pipeline changes weekly. The template doesn't.

A March rating on "objection handling" says nothing about Tuesday's call — the integration-timeline objection that's now come up on three deals in a row.

[Deal coaching](/blog/deal-coaching) works because it attaches to one specific call, close enough to still be honest. A quarterly skills matrix is built for the opposite: distance, at the cost of ever being specific.

## Three things a real plan needs

Not a rating scale. A working document, tied to what's actually open.

Name the deals. Two or three live ones, specific — not "improve discovery," but "the deal where the champion keeps deflecting the budget question."

Set cadence to moments, not slots. A conversation the same afternoon a deal moves. [Sales coaching software](/blog/sales-coaching-software) built around a scheduled review misses that window on purpose, not by accident.

Write the rep's read first. Before anyone else grades the call, note what you noticed. [Sales rep autonomy](/blog/sales-rep-autonomy) is the reason that order matters — a plan that only exists for someone else's use was never really yours.

## Where the rubric came from

Coaching plans got built around HR's calendar, because that's whose review cycle needed a form.

Not the deal's calendar. Not the moment a rep's read was actually sharp.

That's a structural choice. It can be a different one.

## The plan that finally got used

Marcus rewrote his. Five skill categories became two named deals — one at risk, one close to closing.

He noted something after every call that touched either one. Three weeks later, his manager asked what had changed.

Nothing about his objection handling. He'd just started tracking the deals instead of the score.
