"Is this being recorded?"

The question lands mid-sentence, usually right after something the
customer actually cares about. A pricing concern. A real objection.

What happens in the next five seconds decides more than the rep
realizes.

## The question isn't about the technology

Customers aren't asking for a technical explanation. They're asking
whether they can keep talking freely.

A hedge — "it's just for internal notes," said while looking away —
answers a different question than the one they asked. It tells them
something is happening that the rep would rather not detail.

That reads as evasion, even when nothing improper is going on. The
customer stops volunteering the thing they were about to say.

## Vague answers cost more than honest ones

The instinct is to minimize. Downplay the tool, wave off the question,
move back to the pitch.

It backfires because minimizing implies there's something to minimize.
[We've written before](/blog/gong-alternative-without-recording) about
what a recording actually is once it exists: an artifact that outlives
the conversation, sits on a server the customer never sees, and can be
pulled up by someone who wasn't in the room.

A customer who senses that — even without naming it — reacts to the
vagueness, not the AI.

## What actually earns the trust back

The fix isn't a longer disclosure. It's a shorter, truer one.

Say exactly what the tool does. Say exactly what it doesn't do. Stop
talking.

"It's a note for me, not a recording — nobody else sees it, and it's
not saved as a call transcript." That's a full answer. It's specific
enough to check, and specificity is what a vague answer can never
fake.

## Why the honest version is easier to defend

A rep who's vague has to remember the vague story and keep it
consistent across every follow-up question.

A rep telling the truth doesn't have that problem. There's only one
version of what's actually happening, and it doesn't change no matter
how the customer probes it.

That's the real advantage of a tool built not to record in the first
place: there's nothing to explain away later, because there's nothing
sitting on a server that contradicts what the rep said in the room.

## The states where this isn't optional

In two-party consent states, this stops being a trust question and
becomes a legal one — [the rules vary enough by state](/blog/two-party-consent-states-sales-calls)
that "everyone does it" isn't a defense.

Even outside those states, treating disclosure as optional trains
reps to hedge by default. The hedge is the habit that actually costs
deals.

## Build the answer before the question comes

The reps who handle this well aren't more honest by nature. They
decided the answer in advance, so nothing has to be improvised under a
customer's direct gaze.

Know what's true. Say it plainly.

Sara doesn't record, transcribe, or store customer conversations —
the rep's own account afterward is the whole record. That's what makes
the honest answer to "is this being recorded" the reassuring one, not
the risky one.

## Trust is the answer, not the apology

Customers don't need a rep to apologize for using a tool. They need
one who isn't afraid to say what it is.

The AI on the call was never the trust problem. The unwillingness to
describe it plainly always was.
